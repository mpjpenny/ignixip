What the record is for

An SPF record is a TXT record on the domain that sends mail. It names the hosts allowed to use that domain as the envelope sender, which receivers see as the Return-Path. RFC 7208 is the specification. The record does not mention the From header. DMARC is what later compares the two. A domain that never sends mail should still publish a record that ends in -all, so receivers can reject anyone who tries.

Look at the live record with the DNS lookup (TXT on the bare domain) or the mail authentication check, which explains the same record in sentences. The check you read must be the one in DNS right now. A copy in a hosting panel can be wrong if it was never published, or if a second system also writes TXT records.

How a record is read

A record starts with v=spf1 and then a list of mechanisms. Evaluation stops at the first match. The mechanisms you will actually publish:

  • ip4:203.0.113.10 and ip4:203.0.113.0/24 match a sending address directly. ip6: does the same for IPv6. Neither one needs a DNS lookup at evaluation time.
  • a matches the A or AAAA records of the domain. mx matches the addresses of its MX hosts. Both cause lookups.
  • include:example.net evaluates example.net's SPF record and matches if that record passes. It costs one lookup plus whatever the included record does. It does not match just because the name exists.
  • exists: is a lookup used as a yes-or-no test. You rarely need it.
  • all matches everything. It belongs at the end, with a qualifier. Nothing after it is read.

Do not publish ptr. RFC 7208 says not to: it spends a lookup on a reverse name that is easy to get wrong. Use ip4 or ip6 for addresses you run. redirect= replaces the rest of the record with another domain's record. It is not a second include.

The ten-lookup limit

RFC 7208 section 4.6.4 requires a receiver to do at most ten DNS lookups while evaluating one SPF check. The mechanisms that count are include, a, mx, ptr, and exists, and the redirect modifier. ip4, ip6, and all do not count. Each include counts as one, and then the included record's own mechanisms count as well.

That is why a record with three includes can already be over the limit. Google's include and Microsoft's include each contain further includes. Adding a marketing platform, a helpdesk, and a survey tool on top can push a legal-looking record into permerror. A permanent error means the receiver could not tell whether the sender is authorised. DMARC then cannot use SPF. Some receivers treat the error as a failure on their own.

Prefer fewer includes, DKIM where the service can sign as you, and ip4 or ip6 for servers you control. Copying a provider's addresses into your record avoids their lookups, then goes stale when they renumber. After every edit, count the nested lookups again. The mail authentication check shows the record that is actually published.

~all and -all

The character in front of all is the result for every sender that matched nothing earlier.

  • -all is a fail. Everyone not named above is unauthorised. Publish this when the list of senders is complete. It is also the right ending for a domain that should not send mail at all.
  • ~all is a soft fail. The sender is not in the list, but you are not asking for a hard rejection from SPF alone. It is reasonable while you are still discovering old senders. It does not authorise them. A soft fail is not an SPF pass, so it does not satisfy DMARC.
  • ?all is neutral. You are refusing to say. It does not help a receiver decide.
  • +all is a pass for the entire internet. Do not publish it. It tells every receiver that any host may send as you.

Moving from ~all to -all is safe once you have watched mail for a while and every legitimate source is either named in SPF or signing DKIM with your domain. Do the move on its own, without other DNS edits the same day, so a mistake is obvious.

Two SPF records

A domain must have one TXT record that starts with v=spf1. If evaluation finds more than one, RFC 7208 says the result is permerror. The second record does not add to the first. Receivers do not merge them.

This happens when two panels both "help". The old host still serves a record, and the new host publishes another. Or someone adds Microsoft beside Google by creating a second TXT instead of editing the first. Delete until one remains, with every sender inside it:

v=spf1 include:_spf.google.com include:spf.protection.outlook.com -all

Google documents include:_spf.google.com for Workspace in their SPF setup guide. Microsoft 365 documents include:spf.protection.outlook.com in their SPF configuration guide. Copy the include from the provider's current document if you are setting that product up. Do not stack a second record beside it.

Other TXT records on the zone are fine. A DMARC record lives at _dmarc, not on the apex. A site-verification token does not start with v=spf1 and is not a second SPF record. Only a second v=spf1 string is the mistake.

Records you can copy

One static mail server, and nobody else. 203.0.113.10 is a documentation address. Replace it with the server's real address.

v=spf1 ip4:203.0.113.10 -all

Mail only from a provider, while you confirm nothing else sends:

v=spf1 include:_spf.google.com ~all

A domain that must not send:

v=spf1 -all

Publish the record on the domain used in the Return-Path. If the envelope sender is bounce.example.com, that name needs the record, not only example.com. Then check the TXT response from a resolver that is not your own office cache. The DNS lookup on this site asks a public resolver from your browser, which avoids a stale internal view.

When the next service will not fit under ten lookups, sign it with DKIM aligned to the From domain and leave it out of SPF.
